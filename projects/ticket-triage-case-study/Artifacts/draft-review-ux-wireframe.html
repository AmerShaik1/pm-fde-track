1Recognition over recall. The customer's original message is shown again directly above the draft — the merchant never has to remember or scroll back to check what was actually asked before approving a reply to it.
2Visibility of system status. "Not sent yet — awaiting your review" removes any ambiguity about whether an action already happened — critical for a tool merchants are still building trust in.
3User control and freedom. Three distinct actions, not just "Approve" — Edit and Reject are equally visible, not buried, so the merchant never feels like the only affordance is agreeing with the AI.
4Error prevention over error correction. The refund/damaged ticket (needs judgment) is visibly tagged "Needs You — Not Automated" in the list itself — the merchant can't accidentally mistake it for something safe to fast-approve, before they even open it.
5Affordance clarity. Approve is solid/colored (signals the primary, safe action); Reject uses a red outline, not a filled red button — visible and easy to find, but not visually shouting "danger" for a normal, expected action.
6The confidence score, placed deliberately. Sits next to the ticket subject, not competing with the draft text itself — visible before you read the draft, quiet enough not to dominate the review.
